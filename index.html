Rethinking the Patient Experience in Dentistry

Dental anxiety is more common than many think and is one of the biggest barriers to consistent business from patients in dental care. This website guides dentists seeking to enter the market or are already in the market on how to properly communicate, create a comfortable and more accessible environment, and improve patient experience. This guide draws from literature and medicine focusing on firsthand observations, patient narratives, and disability studies.

Why Patients Fear Dentistry and Why That Fear Persists

The Scale of the Problem

Research has revealed that a sizable portion of the patient population has anxiety ranging from moderate to high. A decent chunk that experiences anxiety is classified as having dental phobia. This results in many patients avoiding dental care for years at a time.

The Root of the Problem

Fear as a Systemic Design Flaw

Dental anxiety is the number barrier for inconsistent patient attendance. It is mistaken that pain is a major concern for anxiety and fear, but reality is that pain is only a fraction of the experience many endure. Long lasting negative emotions connected to dental practices are linked to uncertainty, loss of control, and unpleasant interactions. Most of these feeling are introduced in childhood and usually are reinforced over time through similar interactions.

In dental practices anxiety is typically treated as an external factor rather than an internal systematic design flaw. Patients are sometimes reassured through vague response from the dentist or receive little to no reassurance. Dentist may commonly say that the procedure "won't hurt" or is "almost done" to reassure the patient, but the truth is that type of communication is not as effective as well-spoken explanations. Over time vague communication can lead to broken trust and patients will begin to expect unpleasant experiences for future appointments.

Key Takeaway

The dental providers inability to reassure effectively and the patient's reality is a reliable predictor for long term dental avoidance. Trust comes from well-spoken preparation and explanation.

Efficiency Over Experience: The Default Model

From my firsthand experience working in a pediatric dentist/orthodontics office, I can reasonably say that most dental offices are designed for efficiency rather than emotional comfortability. Staff and procedure explanations are not commonly structured with patient accessibility or feelings in mind.

Common Structural Triggers of Dental Anxiety

Sources of Anxiety How It Appears in Practice Patient Experience

How Fear Becomes Chronic

Chronic dental anxiety is rarely developed from a single unpleasant experience. It is understood as cycle of negative reinforcement; in other words, a negative narrative is formed after each negative experience.

Applying Literature & Medicine to Dental Practice

Insights

The concepts from this page are drawn from medical humanities, narratives, and disability studies.

Concept #1

The Medical Model vs. The Social Model

One of the most important topics in medicine is the difference between the medical model and the social model of healthcare. The medical model focuses on identifying the underlying issue and finding a "fix" by treating the condition. The social model focuses on aspects like the environment, communication, and structure, and how they are limiting.

In dentistry, the shift from the medical model to the social model requires recognition of how fear and anxiety are shaped from the system and not a natural trait of the patient. Patients who avoid returning to the dentist are typically just responding to prior experiences that were overwhelming.

Category Medical Model Approach Social Model Approach
Focus Diagnosing and treating the condition Improving the environment, communication, and patient experience
View of the Patient Focused on the condition Recognizing the patient's preferences, emotions, and experiences
Source of Barriers Patient's anxiety/fear or non-compliance Environmental and Communication barriers
Intervention Procedure; sedation Structural redesign of communication and environment
Concept #2

The Medical Gaze

The concept of the medical gaze explains how patients are typically viewed as a diagnosis or procedure which is very surface level rather than a being with emotions and an identity that is driven through personal narratives. When dental providers focus on efficiency rather than engagement, patients are more likely to feel misunderstood or unheard.

Concept #3

Language, Metaphor, and Patient Perception

Words can carry a lot of power and meaning behind them, sometimes more than we realize. The use of language and metaphors can shape a patient's perception and not always in a healthy way. As discussed in Susan Sontag's Illness as a Metaphor, the way we approach illness and treatment can influence how people respond to their experience.

In dentistry, language that implies pain or urgency can increase the patient's anxiety, and unclear explanations can create some confusion. It is advised to use more neutral language that can help patients feel more in control of their situations.

Examples of Patient Communication

  • "This might be uncomfortable" — Vague; leave patient unprepared
  • "Just a little pinch" — Minimizing; Loss of trust if sensation exceeds "little"
  • "We need to fix this immediately" — Urgency can trigger a threat response; can lead to avoidance
  • "You may feel some pressure here, it will last about ten seconds" — Specific, accurate, allows patient to prepare themselves
  • "We're going to work on this together" — Patient feels like they have support and are not alone in this

Examples of Metaphors

  • "Your teeth are decaying" — leads to hopelessness; fear
  • "Your teeth are in really bad shape" — causes embarrassment; shame
  • "You've neglected this area" — feelings of judgment
  • "This is something we can improve together" — Encourages collaboration and support rather causing fear
  • "We're going to take this step by step" — Helps the patient feel more in control of the situation
Concept #4

Patient Narratives and Past Experiences

The personal stories patients have lived through that are built around their experiences in dental offices are key for understanding and predicting patient behavior. As discussed in Eli Clare's Brilliant Imperfection, experiences shape how individuals perceive themselves and the systems around them. A single negative experience can be what starts the cycle and influence behaviors that can reinforce future experiences.

Common Patient Narratives

"I desperately need to go to the dentist but I have severe trauma from a specific incident and subsequent visits after that. Does anyone know of any trauma informed and VERY non-judging dentists?" – Anonymous
"I'm on the verge of a panic attack ... I had ONE dentist that would keep telling me what she's doing at all times and it was so helpful." – Anonymous

Improving the Patient Experience in Dentistry

The Three Core Patient Support Systems

Anxiety Screening
Identify underlying issues prior to appointment through online questionnaires

System #1

Anxiety Screening

A completed anxiety screening prior to appointments helps determine causes of patients past dental experiences so dental providers can approach patients in ways tailored to their specific needs.

Anxiety Screening Question Examples

  • Prior experience: "How would you describe your past experiences with dental care?"
  • Fears: "Are there any aspects of dental care that cause you anxiety? (e.g., needles, sounds, bright lights, loss of control)"
  • Communication preferences: "Would you prefer step-by-step explanations, or would you prefer that we proceed with the procedure."
  • Stop signal: "Do you have a preferred signal or way of letting us know if you need a break at any time"
System #2

The Five Minute Protocol

First impressions influence how patients perceive your dental business. The "Five Minute Protocol" focuses on effectively greeting patients, acknowledging, and comforting patients based off their screening.

  1. Acknowledge by Name
    Greet the patient by their name immediately when they arrive because names are much more personal. For example, "Hey Sarah! Are you excited you're getting braces today? How do you feel about it?"
  2. Review Intake
    Review and mention any concerns on the anxiety screening with the patient to make sure that they know they have been acknowledged. For example, "Hey Sarah, I see you mentioned your bad with needles, I will make sure our team knows and they will properly address it with you."
  3. Provide Orientation
    Explain the appointment structure. For example, "Today, we will start with a check-in, then we will walk you through everything that is happening before we start. The appointment will take about 30 minutes."
  4. Stop Signal Reminder
    You want to remention the stop signal that was noted on the intake, so you can make sure the patient and the team are on the same page. For example, "Remember, you can raise your hand at any moment, and we will take a break, don't be worried if you must stop frequently."
  5. Transition to Treatment
    Walk the patient to their procedural area and mention to them that the dentist will go over everything with them. For example, "I'll walk you over to your room, while you wait for Dr. Smith would like to watch anything."
System #3

The Treatment Preview

The treatment preview helps prepare patients by reducing uncertainty and making the patients feel less overwhelmed before the procedure begins.

Topic What to Include Examples
Sequence Explain the order of the procedure "First I'll apply a numbing gel, then the injection, then we'll wait a few minutes for it to take effect."
Sensation Describe accurately what the patient may feel "You will feel some pressure and vibrations, but you shouldn't feel any pain."
Duration State a time estimate that each step of the procedure may take "The injection takes about 30 seconds. The numbing will be done in about 5 minutes."
Sound Explain what sounds the patient may hear "You will hear the drill, it may sound overwhelming, but you can use headphones if that helps."

Page 5 · Accessibility

Making Dental Care More Accessible

Common Accessibility Barriers

Disabilities Barriers Accommodations
Autism Easily overwhelmed by lights, sounds, touch and sudden changes Quieter environment, hands-on visual procedure walkthrough, sunglasses, headphones
Tourette Syndrome Involuntary movement and speech, fear of judgment Informed staff, longer sessions, option for private room, frequent pausing
Deaf / Hard of Hearing Hard of hearing, verbal communication can't be relied on Written orientations/previews, visual explanations, videos with captions, translator for big procedures
Visual Impairment Issues navigating Hands-on procedure preview, verbal explanations, longer appointments, more guidance

Sensory Environment

Dental offices can become very overwhelming for patients that struggle with sensory sensitivities, which can be seen in disorders like autism, anxiety, Tourette's, hearing, visual, etc. When handling patients with disorders it is important to be mindful of the effect of bright lights, instrument sounds, crowds, and contact.

Creating More Accessible Dental Care

Accessibility within dental care needs to be addressed from the beginning of patient care. If practices wait for the issues to arise first, they are making the situation harder on the patient and themselves, which is ultimately hurting their business more than helping. It is important that each practice has a protocol for adjusting systemic patient care based on the patient and their needs.
